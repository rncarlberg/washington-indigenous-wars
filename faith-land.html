Prophecy, Faith, and Land

Indigenous and missionary visions of the future shaped encounters over belief, homelands, and American settlement on the Columbia Plateau.

← Return to the timeline

The transformation of the nineteenth-century Northwest involved more than newcomers arriving and boundaries changing. It also involved competing claims about whose ways of life deserved protection, who could possess the land, and what obligations settlers owed the peoples already living there.

Samuel Parker defended Indigenous possession of established homelands and condemned American acquisitiveness. Marcus Whitman, writing in 1844, anticipated that white settlers would demand those lands and accepted their displacement of Indigenous people. Both appealed to Providence. Their contrasting arguments show that Christian belief did not dictate a single response to expansion.

When prophetic worlds met

Christopher L. Miller’s Prophetic Worlds adds an essential perspective: missionaries did not enter a spiritual vacuum, and Plateau peoples were not simply passive recipients of a new religion. Indigenous communities interpreted the newcomers, their teachings, and Christianity through their own histories, ceremonies, dreams, prophecies, and expectations about a changing world.

The missionaries also brought a prophetic framework. Many understood their journey as part of Providence and expected conversion, Christian institutions, and American settlement to fulfill a divinely directed future. Miller argues that Plateau peoples and missionaries could initially see one another as important to the fulfillment of their respective destinies. That apparent convergence did not mean that they shared the same beliefs or goals.

This helps explain why early curiosity, exchange, or selective adoption could be mistaken for agreement. When each side’s expectations were disappointed—and as disease, settlement, and demands for land intensified—the relationship deteriorated. Miller’s interpretation does not reduce the history to a misunderstanding or excuse dispossession. It shows how different systems of meaning shaped choices within an increasingly unequal struggle over land and power.

See Christopher L. Miller, Prophetic Worlds: Indians and Whites on the Columbia Plateau.

Samuel Parker: a defense of Indigenous homelands

“I am not able to discover why the nations who have, from time immemorial, occupied this country, and who, like other nations, have their territorial limits tolerably well defined among themselves, should not still possess the domain which our common Creator and Benefactor has kindly given them.”

Parker recognized nations with territorial rights. His warning placed responsibility for their threatened destruction on the conduct of Americans. He hoped divine intervention would restrain that conduct.

Read the longer Parker passage

“It is a subject of increasing regret to every true friend of humanity, that unless the rapacious and acquisitive spirit, which urges our nation to appropriate these western territories, shall be restrained by the providence of God, these Indian nations will be compelled to yield their lands, their rights, and their lives to the merciless invaders of their country. . . . They are inevitably doomed to extinction by the hands of enlightened and powerful men[.] The history of the past, and the operation of present causes, show that as soon as the Indians shall be induced to sell and cede the best portions of their country, there being no farther west to which they can be removed, the Indian race must expire.”

Samuel Parker, Journal of an Exploring Tour Beyond the Rocky Mountains, 4th edition (1844), pp. 270–271. Marcus Whitman: settlement becomes the priority

“I have no doubt our greatest work is to be to aid the white settlement of this country and help to found its religious institutions.”

Whitman still described offering Indigenous people Christian salvation as part of his work. Yet in the same letter he acknowledged that settlers would demand their land before the missionaries’ work could mature. His acceptance of that prospect placed the future of white settlement ahead of the continued possession of Indigenous homelands.

Read the longer Whitman passage

“It does not concern me so much what is to become of any particular set of Indians. . . . I have no doubt our greatest work is to be to aid the white settlement of this country and help to found its religious institutions. Providence has its full share in all these events. Although the Indians have made and are making rapid advance in religious knowledge and civilization, yet it cannot be hoped that time will be allowed to mature either . . . before the white settlers will demand the soil and seek the removal of both the Indians and the Mission. What Americans desire of this kind they always effect, and it is equally useless to oppose or desire it otherwise. . . . Indeed, I am fully convinced that when a people refuse or neglect to fill the designs of Providence, they ought not to complain at the results; and so it is equally useless for Christians to be anxious on their account.”

The first omission includes Whitman’s statement that he wished to offer Indigenous people salvation through the gospel and an opportunity for what he called civilization. That context preserves the tension in his argument: missionary concern for individuals alongside acceptance of their peoples’ displacement.

Marcus Whitman to Narcissa Whitman’s parents, May 16, 1844, Waiilatpu. Read the full letter in an online transcription. The transcription reports light spelling edits. The excerpt here follows the passage supplied by the site editor.

For the historical argument that brought these passages together, see Marc James Carpenter’s The War on Illahee (2025).

Why these voices belong in this story

Read together, these passages reveal a disagreement within the missionary world about the meaning of American expansion. Parker’s criticism demonstrates that contemporaries could recognize dispossession as an injustice. Whitman’s letter shows how a missionary could acknowledge its consequences and still regard accommodation to settlement as his duty. Neither passage alone establishes how its author acted in every encounter, nor does either speak for all Protestant or Catholic missionaries.

Miller’s interpretation widens the view beyond that disagreement among missionaries. It restores Plateau peoples as interpreters and decision-makers whose spiritual traditions shaped the encounter, even as epidemic disease, settler migration, and colonial power sharply constrained their choices.

Their language also has limits. Parker’s prediction of extinction denied Indigenous peoples an open future; it was a fear, not an outcome we should repeat as historical fact. Indigenous nations survived, adapted, resisted, and continued to assert their rights. Their decisions and voices remain central to the history that follows.

These accounts concern the wider Oregon Country, including lands that later became Washington. They help frame the coming struggle over treaties, land, and political authority: the question was not simply whether newcomers would arrive, but whether Indigenous nations could retain their homelands and determine their own futures.
