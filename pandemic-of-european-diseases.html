How epidemic disease and the introduction of alcohol reshaped Indigenous Washington more decisively than any battle.

Effects of European Disease and Alcohol on Indigenous Americans

North America was isolated for at least 10,000 years. Europeans had developed a degree of immunity to European disease over thousands of years. North America was virgin soil. In Native villages, often everyone fell ill at the same time. Because there were no healthy adults left to forage, chop firewood, haul water, or tend to the sick, a massive percentage of the mortality rate was actually caused by starvation, dehydration, and secondary infections rather than the primary disease itself. Historians and anthropologists estimate that between 1780 and 1900, the overall Indigenous population of the Pacific Northwest declined by 80% to 90%. In certain highly vulnerable geographic zones — specifically the riverine valleys of western Oregon and southwestern Washington — the destruction was nearly total, with documented population losses reaching as high as 95% to 97% by the mid-19th century.

The introduction of distilled alcohol to the Pacific Northwest during the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries functioned as a highly disruptive catalyst that altered regional trade, social structures, and diplomacy. Initially, many Indigenous communities were drawn to high-proof spirits not merely for casual consumption, but because the intense, altered states of intoxication were occasionally integrated into existing cultural and spiritual frameworks associated with vision quests and the spirit world. Recognizing the immediate threat this posed to regional stability and the Indigenous workforce, corporate entities like the Hudson’s Bay Company — under Chief Factor John McLoughlin — enforced strict “dry” monopolies across the interior to protect fur yields, while prominent Plateau leaders like Chief Kamiakin fiercely advocated for total abstinence and later mandated strict anti-liquor clauses directly into the treaties of 1855 to secure reservation borders. Despite these internal and external restrictions, independent American “whiskey peddlers” and land speculators systematically flooded the region with low-grade frontier liquor, intentionally weaponizing intoxication during trade and treaty negotiations to cloud judgment, create debilitating economic dependencies, and manipulate tribal leaders into signing away millions of acres of ancestral homelands.

In 1800, after a devastating smallpox epidemic in 1774, Washington’s Indigenous population was about 86,000. They shared 71,000 square miles. By 1900, the remaining Indigenous population had dwindled to roughly 23,000, residing mostly on reservations encompassing just 4,900 square miles. In short: only 25% of the original 1800 population was left, confined to a mere 7% of their ancestral land. In the 1850s there was a series of wars between European migrants and Indigenous tribes across the state, as the first territorial governor, Isaac Stevens, pursued a goal of extinguishing Indigenous title, forcing Indigenous peoples onto reservations and claiming land for white settlers. However, the real work of clearing the land for settlement was done by European disease — smallpox, measles, typhoid, malaria, and more.

Smallpox Epidemic of the 1770s

In the early 1770s, a devastating smallpox epidemic struck the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest, including tribes in what is now Washington State. This was the first known smallpox outbreak in the region and coincided with the first direct European contact. The epidemic caused widespread mortality, killing an estimated 11,000 Indigenous people in western Washington alone, reducing populations from approximately 37,000 to 26,000. The disease spread rapidly along coastal and inland tribes, causing significant demographic collapse not only from the illness itself but also from malnutrition due to the loss of hunters within communities.

The scars and pockmarks from smallpox were visible on survivors for decades, as noted by explorers such as Lewis and Clark. This epidemic marked the beginning of a series of devastating disease outbreaks that profoundly affected Indigenous populations throughout the 18th and 19th centuries.

About this project: I got interested in the subject while serving on the board of the Shafer Museum in Winthrop, WA. The Shafer's focus is the settlement period of the Methow Valley, 1886–1940. There is an unavoidable question of who was here before settlement. I happened on Jack Splawn’s book Kamiakin: The Last Hero of the Yakimas, and the Indigenous history opened up. The question of who was here before settlement is better understood in the context of the whole state or region. This site hopefully answers some of those questions.
