Nearly 16,000 years of human history in the Columbia Basin — from the people of Cooper’s Ferry through ancient Indigenous networks, European contact, treaties, warfare, and survival.

Deep Time: People in the Columbia Basin

The region’s history does not begin with European exploration. Archaeology places people in the Columbia Basin near the end of the Ice Age.

About 16,000 years ago

Cooper’s Ferry: People in an Ice Age World

As the last Ice Age began to loosen its grip, vast ice sheets still covered much of Canada, the Puget Sound lowlands, and parts of the Great Lakes region, Scandinavia, and Britain. Yet people were already living at Cooper’s Ferry on the Salmon River in western Idaho. This was thousands of years before established farming economies or cities, when mammoths, mastodons, giant ground sloths, and now-extinct native horses roamed North America.1

Their early occupation overlapped the era of the great Ice Age floods. Repeated failures of the ice dam holding back Glacial Lake Missoula sent enormous floods across eastern Washington and down the Columbia, carving the Channeled Scablands and reshaping the region.2

Known to the Nez Perce as Nipéhe, the site preserves evidence of repeated occupation over thousands of years. Its earliest dates precede Clovis, challenging the former “Clovis-first” model and contributing to evidence of a longer, more complex peopling of the Americas. Arrival along the Pacific coast, followed by travel inland through the Columbia River system, is one explanation; the site does not establish the precise route.3

Much later, many Indigenous societies in the Midwest and East came to depend heavily on agriculture, including corn, beans, and squash—the “Three Sisters.” Across much of the Pacific Northwest, salmon, other fish, shellfish, game, roots, and berries—carefully harvested, tended, and stored—supported a different path. The Northwest Coast became a striking example of large villages, extensive trade, and complex social institutions sustained without dependence on staple-crop farming.4

Footnotes and further reading

2. Ice Age Floods National Geologic Trail and flood routes and landscape. The flood era overlaps the site’s early occupation; this does not establish that the site itself was inundated or that its inhabitants witnessed a particular flood.

3. Oregon State: Cooper’s Ferry discoveries, dates, and proposed coastal route; site location map. Hearths, tools, animal bones, and extinct horse tooth fragments provide evidence of life at the site; its earliest inhabitants’ exact diet remains uncertain.

4. Later food systems: National Park Service: corn, beans, and squash; Washington archaeology guide; Burke Museum: plant foods and cultivation; Nez Perce roots, berries, and storage. These later societies are not being projected back to 16,000 years ago.
Thousands of Years: Nations, Seasons, and Salmon

Across generations, Indigenous peoples developed distinct cultures finely adapted to the lands and waters of the Northwest.

For millennia before contact

Living with the Seasons

Families returned to known places as foods became available: permanent or repeatedly occupied winter villages; spring root grounds; summer fisheries, gatherings, and trade; and fall hunting, berrying, food preservation, and preparation for winter. This seasonal round was not aimless wandering. It was a knowledgeable and sustainable use of a varied landscape, organized through kinship, custom, stewardship, and inherited rights.

Most peoples of the region did not depend on fields of domesticated crops such as corn, beans, and squash. They nevertheless managed food-producing landscapes intensively: tending fisheries, burning prairies, weeding root grounds, harvesting selectively, and replanting smaller roots. Stored salmon, roots, berries, shellfish, and game supported established villages, extensive trade, and complex social and political life.

Read about Coast Salish villages and seasonal life, explore Washington State University’s account of Plateau foods and root-ground management, or visit the Fort Walla Walla Museum virtual school tour for videos and discussion prompts on Indigenous people and the region’s history.

An enduring connection

Salmon Links Coast and Interior

Salmon connected communities from the coast through the Columbia and its tributaries. Fisheries such as Celilo, Kettle Falls, and Spokane Falls were also centers of trade, ceremony, councils, and visiting. Preserved salmon moved through extensive Indigenous networks, along with tools, materials, knowledge, and stories.

16th Century: The Distant Ripple

No European set foot in the Pacific Northwest during this century, but the forces that would eventually reach it were already in motion elsewhere in the Americas.

c. 1527

Spanish Pacific Voyages Begin

Spanish galleons begin regular voyages between Mexico and the Philippines, establishing a limited Spanish presence in the North Pacific. The colonization of the Americas was already underway elsewhere, setting in motion forces that would eventually reach the Northwest.

17th Century: Indirect Transformations

No direct European contact took place in the Pacific Northwest during this century — but European diseases and goods were already traveling between Native peoples across the continent through established trade networks.

c. 1600s–1730

The Horse Reaches the Plateau

Over this extended period, horses spread into the Columbia Plateau through intertribal trade networks from the south. By the time direct European contact began, Plateau tribes had already been reorganizing their societies around horses as a means of transportation and a measure of wealth for decades.

18th Century: First Contact and the Maritime Fur Trade

Direct contact begins, and with it, the fur trade that would draw European and American ships to the Northwest Coast.

1728 & 1741

Russian Expeditions Reach Alaska

Russian expeditions led by Vitus Bering discover the Bering Strait (1728) and sight Alaska and the Aleutian Islands (1741). Russian interest in Pacific fur resources spurs other European powers to explore the Northwest Coast.

1774

First Documented Contact

Spanish explorer Juan José Pérez Hernández lands at Vancouver Island and meets with Native peoples, commencing brief trade — the first documented European–Indigenous contact on the Northwest Coast. A pair of silver spoons exchanged during this visit later served as proof of Spain’s prior presence in the region.

1775

Spanish Claims of Possession

Spanish expeditions under Bruno de Heceta and Juan Francisco de la Bodega y Quadra sail northward, landing and performing ritual acts of possession at points in present-day Washington and British Columbia, asserting Spanish territorial claims.

1778

Captain Cook at Nootka Sound

British Captain James Cook’s third Pacific expedition makes landfall at Nootka Sound, acquires sea otter pelts, and explores the coastline northward to Alaska. Cook’s crew discovers the extraordinary value of sea otter furs in Asian markets, triggering a rush of British and American traders to the Northwest Coast.

c. 1781

The First Major Smallpox Epidemic

Smallpox strikes the Pacific Northwest, likely spreading from a 1779 outbreak in Mexico City, traveling north via trade networks through the Shoshone, Nez Perce, Walla Walla, and Flathead peoples before reaching the coast. Virgin-soil epidemics typically killed 30% or more of affected populations. Explorers later observed pockmarked survivors among the Clatsop, Chinook, Tlingit, Haida, and numerous other nations. William Clark later wrote of one Chinookan village that the disorder had destroyed their nation.

Saukamapee’s account: Chapter XXII of David Thompson’s Narrative of His Explorations in Western America, 1784–1812 records Saukamapee’s recollections of warfare on foot before firearms, the arrival of guns through northern trade networks, the adoption of horses among the Blackfeet, and the devastating smallpox epidemic of 1781. His account offers an Indigenous perspective on the transformation of the northern Plains. It reaches us through Thompson’s later written record and should be read with that mediation in mind. Read the memoir, Chapter XXII, in the 1916 edition edited by J. B. Tyrrell.

1785–1794

The Maritime Fur Trade Explodes

Twenty-five British ships sail to the Northwest Coast to trade furs. Between 1788 and 1794, fifteen American vessels arrive; from 1795 to 1804, fifty American vessels come to trade, compared to only nine British ships. This trade revolutionizes Indigenous economies and social structures.

1789–1794

The Nootka Sound Affair

Spain sends an expedition to fortify its claim to Nootka Sound and seizes British vessels and crews. The 1790 Nootka Sound Convention resolves the dispute in favor of Britain, and by 1794 Spain decides to withdraw from the Northwest Coast, effectively ceding the field to British and American interests.

1792

Vancouver, Bodega y Quadra, and Gray

British explorer George Vancouver and Spanish explorer Bodega y Quadra meet at Nootka Sound to implement the 1790 Convention, while both explore the coastline extensively. American captain Robert Gray discovers and enters the Columbia River, establishing American commercial interest in the region.

19th Century: Trade, Settlement, and Dispossession

The balance shifts from trade to territorial competition, treaty-making, and war.

By 1800–1801

French Canadian and Iroquois Traders in Kootenai Country

Lewis and Clark did not enter an isolated country. Indigenous traders had long connected the coast, Columbia River, Plateau, Plains, and Great Basin. European-made guns, metal tools, beads, cloth, and other goods traveled through these networks before the American expedition.

A U.S. Forest Service history reports that two French Canadian traders working for the North West Company crossed the Rocky Mountains and spent the winter of 1800–1801 with Kootenai people, probably near Tobacco Plains. The same history places Iroquois traders in the northern Rocky Mountain region by about 1800.

Many of these Haudenosaunee traders came from Kahnawà:ke, Kanesataké, and Akwesasne near Montreal. They operated in a French-speaking fur-trade world and often came from Catholic mission communities. Their Indigenous Catholic practices helped introduce Plateau peoples to elements of Christianity well before permanent Protestant or Jesuit missions were established in the region.

Read the Forest Service history of the early Kootenai and Flathead fur trade.

1804–1806

The Lewis and Clark Expedition

The first thorough overland exploration by Americans crosses into the Pacific Northwest. The expedition establishes positive relations with the Nez Perce, who provide crucial aid, but finds relations strained with Columbia River tribes. It also confirms that coastal tribes already possess European goods obtained through extensive intertribal trade networks.

Learn more in Craig Fehrman’s This Vast Enterprise: A New History of Lewis & Clark, which broadens the familiar account by drawing attention to Indigenous perspectives and the many people on whom the expedition depended.

1807–1811

David Thompson and the North West Company

David Thompson, surveyor and fur trader for the rival British North West Company, crosses the Rockies and spends these years mapping and establishing trading posts throughout the Columbia Plateau — including Kootanae House (1807) and Spokane House (1810) — making him the first European to travel and chart the length of the Columbia River. He reaches the mouth of the Columbia in July 1811, only weeks after Astor’s Pacific Fur Company has already founded Astoria there, narrowly losing the race to claim the river’s mouth for Britain. His detailed maps and journals remain one of the most important records of the Plateau tribes and the geography of the region at first sustained contact.

Explore the David Thompson papers and journals, 1806–1845, held by the Oregon Historical Society Research Library. See also Saukamapee’s account in Chapter XXII of Thompson’s memoir.

1811

The Founding of Astoria

John Jacob Astor’s Pacific Fur Company establishes Fort Astoria at the mouth of the Columbia River — the first permanent American settlement on the Pacific coast, and Astor’s bid to build an American fur-trading empire stretching from the Columbia to New York and on to markets in China.

Learn more in Peter Stark’s Astoria: Astor and Jefferson’s Lost Pacific Empire.

1812–1813

The War of 1812 and the Loss of Astoria

With war underway between the United States and Britain and a British warship reportedly approaching the Columbia, Astor’s partners sell Fort Astoria to the rival British North West Company in 1813 rather than risk its seizure. Renamed Fort George, the post — and with it, control of the Columbia Basin fur trade — passes into British hands. The 1818 Treaty of Ghent and the subsequent Convention of 1818 restore the fort to nominal American sovereignty under a joint-occupation agreement, but the Hudson’s Bay Company, formed from the merger of the North West Company in 1821, remains the dominant commercial power in the region for decades afterward.

1800–1863

Successive Smallpox Epidemics

Outbreaks in 1800–1801, 1824, 1836, 1853, and 1863 sweep through the Pacific Northwest, each killing those born since the previous epidemic who lacked immunity. The 1853 epidemic strikes the lower Columbia River, killing half the Native communities at Chinook and The Dalles. By this time a smallpox vaccine existed but did not reach Indigenous populations.

1810s–1840s

The Land-Based Fur Trade

The land-based fur trade replaces the maritime trade as the dominant European economic presence. The Hudson’s Bay Company and North West Company establish fixed forts and interior trade routes, spreading European goods, epidemic diseases, and new forms of resource exploitation — timber, fish, farmland — deeper into the interior than ships ever could.

Disease and alcohol compounded one another’s effects. Repeated epidemics killed elders, healers, diplomats, and other knowledge holders, diminishing the human capital of Indigenous communities. The destructive trade in distilled alcohol could then weaken families, alliances, and leadership structures. Together these losses reduced the human and institutional capacity available to defend land and sovereignty as settler pressure increased.

1831–1832

Four Nimíipuu Men Journey to St. Louis

Four Nimíipuu men from villages on the upper Clearwater travel more than 2,000 miles with returning fur traders to St. Louis. There they seek William Clark, who had visited their people with the Lewis and Clark expedition and was now superintendent of Indian affairs.

Their precise purpose remains debated. They appear to have sought knowledge of the newcomers’ religion and possibly religious teachers, the Bible, or the source of the spiritual power associated with writing and the “Black Book.” Their interest had developed in a region where French-speaking Catholic Iroquois trappers and other fur-trade people were already sharing Christian ideas.

Wep-tes-tse-mookh Tse-mookh (Black Eagle) and Ka-ow-poo (Man of the Dawn) become ill and die in St. Louis. Ta-weis-se-sim-nihn (No Horns on His Head) dies during the return journey. Heh-yookts Toe-nihn (Rabbit Skin Leggings) reportedly rejoins relatives hunting in western Montana but is killed before returning to his Clearwater homeland. The widely circulated story of their journey later helps inspire Protestant missionary expeditions to the Pacific Northwest.

Read the University of Idaho and Nez Perce Tribe account.

Missions and Spiritual Change (1836–1855)

Protestant and Catholic missionaries enter a region where Indigenous peoples have already encountered Christian ideas through French-speaking Catholic Iroquois and the fur trade. Native leaders respond in different ways: invitation, selective adoption, adaptation, and resistance.

They did not enter a spiritual vacuum. Plateau peoples interpreted Christianity through their own teachings, ceremonies, dreams, and expectations, while missionaries often mistook curiosity or selective adoption for a willingness to abandon older beliefs and seasonal lifeways. Christopher L. Miller argues that these initially overlapping expectations later contributed to profound disappointment and conflict. Learn more about Miller’s Prophetic Worlds.

1836–1847

Whitman at Waiilatpu and Spalding at Lapwai

Marcus and Narcissa Whitman establish a Protestant mission among the Cayuse at Waiilatpu near present-day Walla Walla. Henry and Eliza Spalding continue east and establish another among the Nimíipuu on Lapwai Creek, about twelve miles south of present-day Lewiston. The Spaldings relocate to the mouth of Lapwai Creek in 1838.

Eliza Spalding learns Nimíipuutímt and teaches at the mission. Henry Spalding introduces printing, irrigation, mills, and farming while pressing families to remain near the mission throughout the year. That goal conflicts with a seasonal way of life based on fishing, gathering, hunting, and returning to established places at the proper time.

Read the National Park Service history of the Spalding mission.

1841

Jesuits Establish St. Mary’s Mission

After repeated requests from Bitterroot Salish delegations for “Black Robes,” Jesuit Pierre-Jean De Smet helps establish St. Mary’s Mission in the Bitterroot Valley of present-day Montana. It becomes an early center of Catholic missionary activity in the northern Rocky Mountains.

1842–1846

The Coeur d’Alene Mission Moves to Cataldo

Jesuit Nicolas Point begins the Sacred Heart mission among the Schitsu’umsh (Coeur d’Alene) in 1842, first near the north shore of Lake Coeur d’Alene and then at Mission Point on the St. Joe River. Flooding leads the mission to relocate in 1846 to the Coeur d’Alene River near present-day Cataldo.

The surviving Mission of the Sacred Heart is built there between 1850 and 1853 under Jesuit Antonio Ravalli, with substantial Schitsu’umsh labor. The mission later moves to De Smet in 1877 after the proposed reservation boundaries exclude the Cataldo site.

Read the Idaho State Historical Society account.

1847–1852

Oblate Missions among the Yakama

French Oblates arrive at Fort Walla Walla in 1847 and establish missions along the lower and central Yakima River. Owhi and Kamiakin are among the Yakama leaders who invite Catholic missionaries into their country. Fathers Charles Pandosy and Louis D’Herbomez establish St. Joseph’s Mission at Ahtanum in 1852 near Kamiakin’s summer camp.

The missionaries and Yakama dig irrigation channels and cultivate crops, while Catholic teachings mingle and sometimes conflict with Indigenous beliefs. U.S. troops burn the Ahtanum mission during the Yakama War in 1855; it is reestablished in 1867.

Read the HistoryLink account of St. Joseph’s Mission at Ahtanum.

1854

St. Ignatius in the Mission Valley

Jesuits establish St. Ignatius Mission south of Flathead Lake among the Salish and Kootenai. Together with St. Mary’s, Cataldo, and Ahtanum, it forms part of a growing Catholic mission network extending across the northern Rockies and Columbia Plateau.

1841–1843

The Oregon Trail

The Oregon Trail brings the first large waves of American settlers. The “Great Migration” of 1843 establishes permanent American agricultural settlement in the Willamette Valley, fundamentally shifting the balance from trade relationships to land competition.

1846

The Oregon Treaty

The Oregon Treaty between the United States and Britain establishes the international boundary at the 49th parallel, dividing the Pacific Northwest between American and British/Canadian control and setting the stage for divergent colonial policies on either side of the border.

November 29, 1847

The Whitman Massacre

Cayuse warriors attack the Whitman Mission, killing Marcus and Narcissa Whitman and others. The attack stems from tensions over land encroachment, cultural imposition, and the Whitmans’ perceived failure to stop a measles epidemic that devastated the Cayuse while sparing white settlers.

The Cayuse War (1847–1850)

The deaths at Waiilatpu become the justification for a military campaign, shifting authority in the region from missions toward armed territorial power.

January 1848

Battle of Sand Hollows

Oregon Volunteers clash with Cayuse and Palouse forces near the Umatilla River in one of the first major engagements of the Cayuse War. The volunteers continue toward Waiilatpu, but the conflict becomes a prolonged series of pursuits and skirmishes.

1853

Lo-Kout Guides Theodore Winthrop

At about nineteen, Lo-Kout — then known as Loolowcan — guides the American traveler Theodore Winthrop from Fort Nisqually across the Cascades toward the Yakama country. Winthrop’s published account and Lo-Kout’s later recollection offer sharply different views of their journey. Lo-Kout’s long life provides a human-scale view of travel, resistance, survival, and memory during these turbulent years.

Read the story of Lo-Kout’s life.

1854–1855

The Stevens Treaties

The federal government’s central purpose is to extinguish Indigenous title to the land so Washington Territory can be opened to American settlement. In his 1854 address to the territorial legislature, Governor Isaac Stevens calls for appropriations to “extinguish their title throughout the Territory,” while reserving only the lands considered necessary for Indigenous subsistence.

Stevens then negotiates treaties at Medicine Creek (1854), Point Elliott, Point No Point, Neah Bay, Walla Walla, and with the Yakama and other nations in 1855. Under the federal government’s legal framework, tribes cede more than 100,000 square miles while retaining small reservations and certain rights that were not surrendered, including fishing at “all usual and accustomed places.”

The councils involve profound differences in language, political organization, land ownership, and treaty meaning. Stevens also uses pressure and threats; witnesses at the Walla Walla council record his instruction that leaders who refused to sign would “walk in blood knee deep.”

At some councils, statements passed from English into Chinook Jargon and then into a local Indigenous language. These layers of interpretation could obscure unfamiliar legal terms, but translation alone was not the only problem: the parties also brought different understandings of boundaries, political authority, land use, and which rights a treaty surrendered or retained.

Read the University of Washington history of treaty-making and reservations.

The Yakama and Puget Sound Wars (1855–1856)

Conflict follows disputed treaties, settler encroachment, violence, and incursions into Indigenous lands before the treaties had even been ratified.

October 6–8, 1855

Battle of Toppenish Creek (Haller’s Defeat)

Major Granville Haller’s force is intercepted by Yakama fighters and compelled to retreat. The engagement demonstrates the strength and coordination of Indigenous resistance east of the Cascades.

December 7–10, 1855

Battle of Walla Walla

Oregon volunteers fight a four-day battle against Walla Walla, Palouse, Cayuse, and allied fighters. Walla Walla leader Peo-peo-mox-mox, seized while attempting to negotiate, is killed while in custody during the battle.

January 26, 1856

Battle of Seattle

Indigenous forces attack the young settlement of Seattle. Cannon fire from the U.S. Navy sloop-of-war Decatur helps defend the town.

March 26, 1856

Attack at the Cascades

Indigenous fighters attack settlements and blockhouses at the vital Cascades portage on the Columbia. U.S. reinforcements reopen the transportation route; military commissions later condemn several Indigenous men to death.

The Spokane–Coeur d’Alene–Palouse War (1858)
May–September 1858

The Decisive Spokane War of 1858

After Spokane, Coeur d’Alene, Palouse, and allied fighters defeat Lieutenant Colonel Edward Steptoe’s command at Pine Creek, Colonel George Wright returns with a larger force, long-range rifles, and artillery. His punitive campaign defeats the allied forces, destroys stored foods and hundreds of horses, and ends with coerced surrenders and summary executions. This campaign becomes a decisive turning point in the struggle for control of the Inland Northwest.

September 1860

Jack Splawn Enters Washington Territory

At fifteen, Andrew Jackson “Jack” Splawn crosses the Cascades from Oregon and settles in the Klickitat Valley. He becomes a cattleman, businessman, and public official, while spending decades talking with Indigenous leaders and other witnesses about the wars and the transformation of their country. His life offers a settler’s perspective on the same turbulent period remembered by Lo-Kout.

Read the life of Jack Splawn and how to evaluate his historical account.

1862

A Devastating Epidemic

A Pacific Northwest smallpox epidemic kills thousands across the region — one of the worst outbreaks, striking at a time when Native populations are already severely depleted and under pressure from settlement.

1860s–1895

Smohalla and the Wáashat Renewal

Smohalla, a Wanapum spiritual leader at Priest Rapids, gives new strength to the Wáashat, or Seven Drums, tradition. His teachings combine older Columbia River ceremonies with selected Christian elements and call on Indigenous people to maintain ancestral relationships with the earth, salmon, roots, and seasonal life rather than submit to forced farming and cultural assimilation.

Smohalla advocates spiritual and nonviolent resistance, yet government agents regard the movement as a threat because its followers resist removal, allotment, and the authority of reservation agents. The tradition survives official suppression and remains practiced in Northwest longhouses.

Read the HistoryLink biography of Smohalla.

The Nez Perce and Bannock Wars (1877–1878)

Armed resistance continues as the United States attempts to force Indigenous bands onto reduced reservations.

June 17, 1877

Battle of White Bird Canyon

In the opening battle of the Nez Perce War, Nez Perce fighters decisively defeat two companies of U.S. cavalry. The bands then begin a long fighting retreat while attempting to protect their families.

October 5, 1877

Bear Paw

After traveling more than 1,100 miles through present-day Idaho, Wyoming, and Montana, the Nez Perce bands are stopped near the Bear Paw Mountains, roughly 40 miles from Canada. Following a five-day siege, Hinmatóowyalahtq̓it (Chief Joseph) agrees to cease fighting. Survivors are sent far from their homeland.

July 8, 1878

Battle of Birch Creek

During the Bannock War, U.S. forces attack Bannock and Paiute people near Birch Creek in eastern Oregon. The fighting forms part of the final major regional war arising from confinement, hunger, and pressure on Indigenous lands and resources.

1880s–1890s

The Allotment Era Begins

Policies break up communal reservation lands into individual parcels and open “surplus” lands to white settlers. This further erodes tribal land bases, traditional governance, and communal ways of life that had already been severely disrupted by nearly a century of European and American contact.

Summary of Impact

By the end of the 19th century, the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest had experienced:

  • Catastrophic population loss — perhaps up to 90% in some areas due to successive smallpox and other epidemics
  • Economic transformation — from self-sustaining regional economies and extensive Indigenous trade networks toward growing dependence on European goods and participation in global fur markets
  • Political and military subjugation — treaties, wars, and reservation confinement
  • Cultural disruption — missionary pressure, language loss, and the suppression of ceremonies like the potlatch
  • Land dispossession — from sovereign territory to reservations, with the “usual and accustomed” fishing rights reserved in treaties becoming a cornerstone of ongoing legal and cultural struggle

The period from 1774 to 1900 transformed the Pacific Northwest from a region where Indigenous nations had thrived for millennia into one where their survival depended on resistance, adaptation, and the defense of treaty rights that continue to be contested today.
